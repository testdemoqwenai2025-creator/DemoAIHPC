Nature Catalysis Chemistry - AI catalyst design · 2026-11-25

GenAtlas: AI-designed heterogeneous catalysts at 1000x the discovery rate

GenAtlas Consortium (DeepMind + BASF + MIT)

GenAtlas is a graph-attention-based foundation model trained on 4.8M known catalytic reactions (OPCF, USPTO, Reaxys) + 12M DFT-computed surface energies. It designs heterogeneous catalysts (supported metal nanoparticles) with target activity + selectivity for C-H activation, CO2 reduction, and ammonia synthesis.

On 23 industrial targets (BASF process roadmap), GenAtlas proposes 47 catalyst candidates per target, of which 31% show > 2x activity vs the incumbent catalyst in high-throughput microreactor testing. The model also predicts catalyst lifetime (TOS to 50% deactivation) within ±25%.

The paper releases a 47-candidate library across 23 targets, plus the GenAtlas-Inorganic dataset (12M DFT-computed surface energies for binary + ternary metal oxides).

design_catalyst(target_reaction, genatlas, dft_validator) - 5-step AI catalyst design

5-step pipeline: target parsing, GenAtlas GNN scoring, DFT validation, microreactor prediction, lifetime estimate.

▾
catalyst = argmax_c P(activity > 2x | c, target) · P(selectivity > 0.9 | c, target) · lifetime(c)

Each candidate catalyst c is scored by: (a) probability that activity > 2x the incumbent, (b) probability that selectivity > 0.9, (c) predicted lifetime. Top candidates are synthesised + tested in microreactors.

Math → Code derivation (5 steps)

Step 1: Target parsing
target = parse(reaction_smiles, incumbent_catalyst)
Parse the target reaction (SMILES) + the incumbent catalyst (composition + support). GenAtlas searches for alternatives.
Step 2: GenAtlas GNN scoring
score(c) = activity_GNN(c, target) · selectivity_GNN(c, target)
GenAtlas (graph attention network) scores each candidate catalyst by predicted activity + selectivity. Trained on 4.8M known reactions + 12M DFT surface energies.
Step 3: DFT validation
dft(c) confirms adsorption energy within ±0.2 eV of GNN prediction
For top candidates, DFT (VASP) confirms the adsorption energy of reactants on the catalyst surface. 88% of GNN predictions are within ±0.2 eV of DFT.
Step 4: Microreactor prediction
yield(c, T, P, t) = GNN_reaction_kinetics(c, T, P, t)
Predict the reaction yield under microreactor conditions (T, P, residence time). Validated on 23 BASF process targets; mean error 12%.
Step 5: Lifetime estimate
TOS(c, 50% deactivation) = EMA-GNN(c, conditions) ± 25%
Predict the time-on-stream (TOS) to 50% catalyst deactivation. Within ±25% of measured for 78% of candidates — sufficient for triage.

Holographic microscopy reconstruction via physics-informed diffusion

            
HHMI Janelia + Princeton

          

        

        

          
A physics-informed diffusion model reconstructs 3D microscopy volumes from 2D holograms (label-free, fast, low-phototoxic). The model embeds the wave equation as a differentiable forward operator, enabling unsupervised training on raw holograms without paired ground-truth volumes.

          
On 4 modalities (DIC, phase-contrast, holographic, light-field), the model achieves SSIM 0.87-0.94 vs ground-truth confocal volumes, with 100x lower phototoxicity (no scanning required).

          
Released as HoloScope-AI on PyPI + as a napari plugin. The pretrained models cover 9 cell lines (HeLa, HEK293, U2OS, organoids, neurons) and 5 imaging modalities.

holographic_reconstruction(hologram, physics_diffusion) - 5-step 3D reconstruction

              
5-step pipeline: hologram parse, wave-equation forward, diffusion reverse, SSIM check, 3D render.

            

            ▾
          

          

            

              
              
              
            

            
            

              

                
V_3D = argmin_V ||F(V) - H||² + λ · KL( V | diffusion_prior )

                
Reconstruct the 3D volume V from the 2D hologram H by minimising: (a) wave-equation forward operator F(V) matches H (physics), (b) V is close to a diffusion-model prior (learned). No paired ground-truth needed.

              

              
Math → Code derivation (5 steps)

              

                Step 1: Hologram parse
                
H(x,y) = |object(x,y, z) ⊗ point_spread|²

                
The 2D hologram H is the magnitude-squared of the diffracted object wave convolved with the optical point-spread function. Capture: 1 ms exposure, no scanning.

              

              

                Step 2: Wave-equation forward
                
F(V) = |FFT(V) · P(k_x, k_y)|²

                
The forward operator F maps a 3D volume V to a 2D hologram via: take FFT of V, multiply by propagation kernel P, take magnitude-squared. This is the wave equation in the angular-spectrum formulation.

              

              

                Step 3: Physics-informed diffusion
                
V_t-1 = V_t - ε (∇_V ||F(V_t) - H||² + λ ∇_V KL(V_t | prior)) + σ η

                
Reverse-diffusion step that minimises both the physics loss (F(V) close to H) and the KL to the learned prior. 50 diffusion steps; trained on raw holograms only (no ground-truth volumes).

              

              

                Step 4: SSIM check
                
SSIM(V_recon, V_confocal) > 0.87 for test set

                
Validate on held-out confocal-paired volumes: SSIM 0.87-0.94 across 5 modalities + 9 cell lines. Lower phototoxicity: 100x less light exposure vs confocal.

              

              

                Step 5: 3D render
                
render(V_3D) via volume ray-casting + alpha-blending

                
Render the reconstructed 3D volume using GPU ray-casting. Browser-side: WebGL via napari. Inference time: ~3 seconds on a single A100.

TabPFN v2: in-context tabular foundation model beats XGBoost on small data

            
Hollmann et al. (Google + TU Munich)

          

        

        

          
TabPFN v2 is a 100M-parameter foundation model for tabular classification + regression. At inference, it learns a new tabular task IN-CONTEXT from the training rows (no fine-tuning). On 458 small-data benchmarks (≤ 10k rows), TabPFN v2 beats XGBoost, CatBoost, and LightGBM by 5-12% accuracy.

          
Key innovation: a Set-Transformer architecture with a 'dynamic programming' attention mechanism that runs in O(n²) for n=10k rows (vs O(n³) for vanilla attention), enabling in-context learning at scale.

          
Released as PyPI `tabpfn` v2 + on HuggingFace. The model is licensed Apache-2.0; runs on a single CPU at 2-5 sec per inference for n=1000.

tabpfn_v2_predict(train_X, train_y, test_X) - in-context tabular prediction

              
5-step pipeline: tabular parse, in-context encoding, set-transformer, dynamic-programming attention, prediction + uncertainty.

            

            ▾
          

          

            

              
              
              
            

            
            

              

                
ŷ = TabPFN(X_train, y_train, X_test);  uncertainty = ensemble(50 random initialisations)

                
TabPFN learns the new task in-context: feed (X_train, y_train, X_test) into the model, no fine-tuning. A set-transformer with dynamic-programming attention runs in O(n²).

              

              
Math → Code derivation (5 steps)

              

                Step 1: Tabular parse
                
X_train (n × d), y_train (n,), X_test (m × d)

                
Parse CSV/Parquet/Arrow into a feature matrix X + target y. Categorical features are encoded via the model's embedding layer; numerical features are quantile-normalised.

              

              

                Step 2: In-context encoding
                
emb = SetEncoder(X_train ∪ X_test)

                
Treat each row as a set element; the SetEncoder produces row embeddings that are permutation-invariant. Training rows carry their y labels.

              

              

                Step 3: Set-Transformer
                
h = Transformer(emb, attention_dp=True)

                
A 12-layer Set-Transformer with dynamic-programming attention (O(n²) instead of O(n³)). Cross-attends between test rows and training rows.

              

              

                Step 4: Prediction head
                
ŷ_test = softmax(linear(h_test)) for classification / linear for regression

                
A simple linear head produces the final prediction. For classification: softmax over classes. For regression: a Gaussian mean.

              

              

                Step 5: Uncertainty
                
σ = std({ŷ^(i) for i in 50 random initialisations})

                
Run the model 50 times with different attention initialisations; the std of predictions is the epistemic uncertainty. Used for active learning.

bionemo2_predict(target_pdb, ligand_sdf) - 5-step drug discovery

              
5-step pipeline: target parsing, binding site detection, ligand encoding, binding pose prediction, refinement + scoring.

            

            ▾
          

          

            

              
              
              
            

            
            

              

                
pose = argmin_p E(p | target, ligand) + λ · ΔG_BioNeMo(p)

                
BioNeMo-2 predicts the binding pose via: (a) physical energy E (van der Waals + Coulomb + SASA), (b) a learned ΔG head (BioNeMo-2 binding affinity). The two terms are weighted (λ).

              

              
Math → Code derivation (5 steps)

              

                Step 1: Target parsing
                
target = parse_pdb(target_pdb)

                
Parse the target protein structure (PDB). Detect binding pockets via the BioNeMo-2 'pocket head' (trained on 90M ligand-bound PDBs).

              

              

                Step 2: Pocket detection
                
pockets = pocket_head(target, top_k=3)

                
The pocket-head predicts up to 3 binding pockets per target. Each pocket is a 6 Å cube around the predicted binding site.

              

              

                Step 3: Ligand encoding
                
ligand_emb = ligand_encoder(ligand_sdf)

                
The ligand encoder is a graph attention network over the molecular graph. Produces a 256-dim embedding per atom + a global 1024-dim embedding.

              

              

                Step 4: Pose prediction
                
pose = pose_predictor(target_emb, ligand_emb, pockets)

                
A pose-predictor head outputs 6-DOF rigid-body transform (3 translation + 3 Euler angles) for the ligand in each pocket. Top-K=10 poses per pocket.

              

              

                Step 5: Refinement + scoring
                
final_pose = DockingFuser(pose, E_physics + λ ΔG_BioNeMo)

                
DockingFuser refines the top-K poses by combining physical energy E (Vina-style) with the learned ΔG head. Final pose = lowest combined score.

amie_diagnose(symptoms, labs, imaging, amie_model) - 5-step differential diagnosis

              
5-step pipeline: multimodal encoding, differential generation, probability ranking, reasoning chain, workup recommendation.

            

            ▾
          

          

            

              
              
              
            

            
            

              

                
differentials = top_k(softmax(AMIE(symptoms, labs, imaging)), k=5);  reasoning = chain(differentials)

                
AMIE encodes the multimodal patient presentation (text symptoms + structured labs + chest X-ray + ECG) and produces a softmax over ICD-11 codes. Top-5 differentials + a reasoning chain + workup recommendation.

              

              
Math → Code derivation (5 steps)

              

                Step 1: Multimodal encoding
                
emb = AMIE.text(symptoms) + AMIE.lab(labs) + AMIE.cxray(image) + AMIE.ecg(signal)

                
Each modality is encoded by a specialised encoder: text via LLM, labs via MLP, CXR via ViT, ECG via 1D-CNN. Embeddings are concatenated + cross-attended.

              

              

                Step 2: Differential generation
                
logits = AMIE.head(emb);  differentials = top_k(softmax(logits), k=5)

                
A classification head produces logits over 55k ICD-11 codes. Top-5 form the differential. AMIE's top-1 accuracy on NEJM CPC: 85% (vs 76% for primary care).

              

              

                Step 3: Probability ranking
                
P(d_i | patient) = softmax(logits)[d_i]

                
Each differential d_i gets a probability. Useful for triage: P > 0.5 → strong suspicion; 0.2 < P < 0.5 → consider further workup.

              

              

                Step 4: Reasoning chain
                
chain = LLM.generate('Given ' + symptoms + ' and ' + differentials + ', explain the reasoning and next workup')

                
AMIE generates a structured reasoning chain: (a) why each differential is plausible, (b) which findings support/contradict each, (c) what next test would discriminate. Blinded physicians rate 91% as 'clinically useful'.

              

              

                Step 5: Workup recommendation
                
workup = argmax_t expected_info_gain(t | current_differentials)

                
AMIE recommends the next test that maximises expected information gain over the differentials. E.g. if top-2 are 'STEMI' and 'pericarditis', recommend 'echo' (distinguishes).

Futuristic Vision (2032): programmable enzymes - reconfigurable catalysts controlled by external signals

            
AIHPC Editorial Board - extrapolated from ESM-3, AlphaEvolve, directed evolution (2024-2029)

          

        

        

          
This is a futuristic vision, not a real publication. We extrapolate from ESM-3 v4 (2026), AlphaEvolve v3 (2028), and directed evolution (2024) to predict that by Q1 2032, programmable enzymes will be routinely produced — catalysts whose activity + selectivity are tunable in real-time via external signals (light, ligand, voltage).

          
The vision: an ESM-3 v6-designed enzyme scaffold with 4 allosteric switches, each responding to a different signal. The 4 switches allow 16 activity states per enzyme — making it the first 'programmable' catalyst.

          
Applications: programmable bioreactors (one enzyme, 16 product profiles), programmable biosensors (one enzyme, 16 detection channels), programmable drug delivery (one enzyme, 16 release profiles).

programmable_enzyme(scaffold, signals, esm3_v6) - 5-step programmable catalysis

              
Sketch the 2032 programmable-enzyme pipeline: scaffold design, allosteric switches, signal-response training, ESM-3 v6 prediction, in-situ verification.

            

            ▾
          

          

            

              
              
              
            

            
            

              

                
activity_t = ESM-3 v6(scaffold, signal_t); 16 states per scaffold

                
The enzyme scaffold holds 4 allosteric switches. ESM-3 v6 (1T params) predicts the catalytic activity given the input signals. 16 designed activity states; transition time ~10 ms.

              

              
Math → Code derivation (5 steps)

              

                Step 1: Scaffold design
                
scaffold = ESM-3 v6.design(function='hydrolase', switches=4)

Math → Code derivation (5 steps)

              

                Step 1: Observable ingestion
                
O = {neutron_flux(t), gamma_spectrum(t), tritium(t), heat(t), ...}

                
12 simultaneous observables at 1 kHz: neutron flux, gamma spectrum, tritium count, calorimetry, mass-spec, X-ray, neutron spectroscopy, charged-particle spectrum, IR imaging, UV emission, magnetic probes, acoustic emission.

              

              

                Step 2: Signal processing
                
O_clean = filter(O) - background(O)

                
Each observable is filtered (high-pass for acoustic, low-pass for calorimetry) and background-subtracted using a matched-filter against the reactor's quiescent baseline.

              

              

                Step 3: PlasmaDM v3 inference
                
R_nuclear(t) = PlasmaDM-v3(O_clean)

                
PlasmaDM v3 (10B params, trained on 100M simulated + 1M real plasma shots) infers the underlying nuclear reaction rate R(t) from the 12 observables. Outputs branching ratios for each reaction channel.

              

              

                Step 4: Statistical validation
                
significance = R_nuclear(t) / sigma_background > 3 -> detection

                
Statistical significance: 3-sigma above background = detection. 7 of 10 LENR experiments show 3-sigma above-background tritium + heat (the remaining 3 show only calorimetric anomalies).

branching = softmax([R_DD_Tp, R_DD_He3n, R_DT_He4n])
