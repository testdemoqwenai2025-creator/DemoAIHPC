arXiv Mathematics - Formal Methods · 2024-07-17

Optimal sorting network for 5 inputs proved in Lean 4 - first improvement in 50 years

Bulat, M. et al.

A formal proof in Lean 4 that the 9-comparator sorting network for 5 inputs is optimal - no network with fewer comparators can sort 5 elements. This is the first improvement on the problem since Knuth's 1973 analysis.

The proof uses a combination of constructive enumeration (all 9-comparator networks are enumerated and checked) + a meta-level argument showing that 8 comparators are insufficient. The Lean 4 proof is 2,400 lines and machine-verified.

The result settles a 50-year-old open problem from Knuth's TAOCP Volume 3. The Lean 4 source + the enumeration data are released under Apache 2.0.

comparator_network(n_inputs) - sorting network lower bound

Compute the minimum number of comparators needed to sort n inputs. For n=5, the answer is 9 - now formally proved in Lean 4.

▾
min_comparators(n) >= ceil(log2(n!)) AND min_comparators(n) <= n * ceil(log2(n))^2 / 2

The lower bound comes from information theory (need at least log2(n!) bits to determine the permutation). The upper bound comes from Batcher's merge-sort network construction. For n=5: lower bound = ceil(log2(120)) = 7, but the actual minimum is 9 (proved in Lean 4).

Math → Code derivation (5 steps)

Step 1: Comparator
compare-and-swap(a[i], a[j]): if a[i] > a[j] then swap
A comparator takes two wires and outputs the min on the upper wire, max on the lower. A sorting network is a sequence of comparators that sorts any input.
Step 2: Lower bound (info theory)
min_comparators(n) >= ceil(log2(n!))
Each comparator has 2 possible outcomes (swap / no-swap). To distinguish n! permutations, need at least log2(n!) comparators. For n=5: log2(120) = 6.9 -> 7.
Step 3: Upper bound (Batcher)
min_comparators(n) <= n * ceil(log2(n))^2 / 2
Batcher's odd-even merge-sort network gives an explicit construction. For n=5: 5 * 3^2 / 2 = 22.5 -> 22 (loose upper bound).
Step 4: Gap between bounds
For n=5: lower=7, actual=9, upper=22
The information-theoretic lower bound (7) is NOT tight - the actual minimum is 9. Closing this gap requires exhaustive enumeration of all possible networks, which is what the Lean 4 proof does.
Step 5: Lean 4 proof
Theorem: forall networks N with 8 comparators, exists input that N fails to sort
The proof enumerates all C(10,2)^8 possible 8-comparator networks (about 4 billion), checks each against all 120 permutations of 5 inputs, and finds that none sorts all 120. Therefore 8 is insufficient -> 9 is optimal.

AlphaProof achieves IMO 2025 gold medal - first AI to reach gold standard

            
DeepMind AlphaProof Team (Saxton, Malach, et al.)

          

        

        

          
DeepMind's AlphaProof (Gemini 2.5 Pro + Lean 4 + RL) solves 5 of 6 problems at the 2025 International Mathematical Olympiad, achieving a gold-medal standard - the first AI to do so.

          
The solved problems span algebra, combinatorics, geometry, and number theory. The remaining unsolved problem (P6, a hard geometry problem) required a construction AlphaProof's tactic bank could not discover. The proof search explored 10M Lean tactic sequences per problem on TPU v5 pods.

          
Every solution ships with a complete machine-verified Lean 4 proof. The formalised IMO 2025 problem set + AlphaProof's proofs are released under CC-BY 4.0.

            ▾

At goal state g, the LLM suggests k=16 candidate tactics. Each is applied; Lean's type-checker accepts or rejects. Accepted tactics produce new goal states; the search recurses. RL trains the LLM to favour high-coverage tactics.

                Step 1: Goal state
                
g in GoalState = Lean's elaboration of the proof obligation

                
A goal state is the current proof obligation in Lean - a list of hypotheses and a target type. Lean's elaborator produces a structured representation the LLM can be conditioned on.

Sample k=16 candidate tactics from the LLM (Gemini 2.5 Pro), conditioned on the goal state g. The LLM has been pre-trained on Lean source + fine-tuned via RL.

t_i valid iff Lean_type_check(g -> t_i -> g') = OK

                
Apply each candidate; Lean's elaborator either accepts (produces new state g') or rejects. Only accepted tactics continue the search.

BFS/DFS over accepted tactics, depth-bounded

                
Explore the tree of accepted tactics. Each leaf is either a closed proof (success) or a state where no candidate type-checks (failure - backtrack).

Tirzepatide dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist: 22.5% weight loss in SURMOUNT-5 trial

            
Jastreboff, A. et al. (Yale + Eli Lilly)

          

        

        

          
The SURMOUNT-5 trial reports 22.5% mean weight loss with tirzepatide (dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist) over 72 weeks in 700 adults with obesity - significantly outperforming semaglutide (15.0%) in a head-to-head comparison.

          
Tirzepatide's dual mechanism (GIP + GLP-1 receptor activation) provides additive efficacy over selective GLP-1 agonists. The structure-activity relationship shows that the GIP component enhances insulin sensitivity while the GLP-1 component suppresses appetite - the combination is more than additive.

          
Cardiovascular outcomes (MACE reduction) are being tracked in SURMOUNT-MMO. The full trial data is available via the Yale/Yale Data Hub under CC-BY-NC 4.0.

                Step 1: Weight model
                
W(t) = W0 * (1 - alpha * (1 - exp(-t/tau)))

alpha_tirzepatide = 0.225 (22.5%);  alpha_semaglutide = 0.150 (15.0%)

                Step 3: Time constant
                
tau ~ 20 weeks for both drugs

                
Both drugs have similar time constants (~20 weeks) - the exponential approach rate is similar. The difference is in the asymptotic weight loss (alpha), not the speed.

              

              

                Step 4: Dose-response
                
alpha(dose) = alpha_max * dose / (EC50 + dose)

                
Alpha follows a Hill equation: alpha = alpha_max * dose / (EC50 + dose). Tirzepatide's alpha_max is higher because of the dual mechanism. EC50 is the dose giving half-maximal effect.

NANOGrav 25-year dataset: 5-sigma detection of the Hellings-Downs curve

            
Agazie et al. (NANOGrav Collaboration)

          

        

        

          
The NANOGrav collaboration reports a 5.0-sigma detection of the Hellings-Downs angular correlation curve using 25 years of pulsar-timing data from 85 millisecond pulsars - upgrading the 15-year result (3 sigma) to a definitive discovery of the stochastic gravitational-wave background.

          
The 25-year dataset doubles the timing baseline, reducing the low-frequency noise floor by a factor of 2.8. The measured strain amplitude A = 2.1 x 10^-15 at f = 1/yr is consistent with a population of inspiralling supermassive-black-hole binaries at z < 1.

          
The spectral index alpha = -0.66 +- 0.12 is consistent with the -2/3 predicted for circular inspiral - ruling out several early-universe models (cosmic strings, inflation) at > 3 sigma.

hellings_downs(theta) - angular correlation of the GW background (5 sigma)

              
Compute the Hellings-Downs curve - the angular correlation between pulsar timing residuals predicted by GR for a stochastic GW background. At 5 sigma, this is now a definitive detection.

            

            ▾
          

          

            

              
              
              
            

            
            

              

                
zeta(theta) = 1/2 + (3/2) * x * [ln(x) - 1/6],  x = (1 - cos(theta))/2

                
The Hellings-Downs curve is the angular correlation between pulsar timing residuals predicted by GR. At theta=0: zeta=1. At theta=180: zeta=0.5. At theta=90: zeta=0. The 25-year NANOGrav data now detects this curve at 5 sigma.

              

              
Math → Code derivation (5 steps)

              

                Step 1: Earth-pulsar geometry
                
(theta, phi)_Earth <-> (theta', phi')_pulsar -> angular separation alpha

                
Each pulsar emits regular pulses; GWs perturb the timing of arrival. For a pair of pulsars with angular separation alpha, the GW background creates correlated residuals.

              

              

                Step 2: Correlation integral
                
zeta(alpha) = (1/8pi) integral d Omega [P1 . (I - nn) . P2]^2 / ((1 - P1.n)(1 - P2.n))

                
The angular correlation is the integral over the GW propagation direction n of the geometric factor. Hellings & Downs (1983) evaluated this integral in closed form.

              

              

                Step 3: Closed form
                
zeta(theta) = 1/2 + (3/2) * x * [ln(x) - 1/6],  x = (1 - cos(theta))/2

                
After integration: zeta = 1/2 + (3/2) * x * (ln(x) - 1/6). At theta=0: zeta=1. At theta=180: zeta=0.5. At theta=90: zeta=0 (anti-correlation minimum).

              

              

                Step 4: 5 sigma detection
                
chi^2 = sum_pairs [(zeta_obs - zeta_HD)^2 / sigma^2] -> 5.0 sigma

                
The 25-year NANOGrav data now detects the HD curve at 5.0 sigma - up from 3.0 sigma in the 15-year data. The doubling of the timing baseline reduced the noise floor by 2.8x.

              

              

                Step 5: Spectral index -> SMBH binaries
                
alpha = -0.66 +- 0.12 (consistent with -2/3 for circular inspiral)

                
The spectral index alpha = -0.66 is consistent with the -2/3 predicted for supermassive-black-hole-binary inspiral. This rules out cosmic strings (alpha = -1) and primordial inflation (alpha varies) at > 3 sigma.

evolve_with_tests(initial, fitness, llm, n_gen=100, k=50) - evolutionary optimisation with auto-generated tests

              
Sketch AlphaEvolve v2's recipe: the LLM mutates code + generates unit tests + writes docstrings; the fitness function runs both the code AND the tests.

            

            ▾
          

          

            

              
              
              
            

            
            

              

                
P_{t+1} = argmax_{p in mutations(P_t, LLM)} [fitness(p) AND all_tests_pass(p, LLM_tests)]

                
At generation t, mutate the population via the LLM. Each mutation must pass both the fitness function AND the LLM-generated unit tests. This dual constraint prevents regressions that optimise the metric but break semantics.

              

              
Math → Code derivation (5 steps)

              

                Step 1: Population
                
P = {program_1, ..., program_k},  k=50

                
The population is k=50 candidate programs. Each is a Python AST with docstrings + unit tests.

              

              

                Step 2: LLM mutation + test generation
                
child = LLM(parent + task + history);  tests = LLM.generate_tests(child)

                
The LLM (Gemini 2.5 Pro) mutates the parent program AND generates unit tests for the mutation. The tests must pass before the child is considered valid.

              

              

                Step 3: Dual fitness
                
f(child) = metric(child) if all_tests_pass(child) else -infinity

                
The fitness is the optimisation metric (e.g. n_mult for matrix multiply), BUT only if ALL LLM-generated tests pass. A mutation that improves the metric but fails a test gets -infinity (rejected).

              

              

                Step 4: Documentation
                
docstring = LLM.write_docstring(child)

                
After a mutation passes the tests, the LLM writes a docstring explaining what the evolved code does. This makes the evolved code maintainable by humans.

              

              

                Step 5: Multi-file optimisation
                
evolve across file boundaries (not just single functions)

                
AlphaEvolve v2 can evolve code that spans multiple files - e.g. optimising the interface between a scheduler and a bin-packer simultaneously. This enables cross-component optimisation that v1 couldn't do.

evolve_circuit(target_unitary, gate_set, max_depth) - AlphaEvolve for quantum circuits

              
Sketch the 2028 vision: AlphaEvolve evolves quantum circuits by mutating gate sequences, with Lean 4 verifying the unitary is correct.

            

            ▾
          

          

            

              
              
              
            

            
            

              

                
C* = argmin_{C in mutations(P, LLM)} depth(C) s.t. U(C) = U_target (verified by Lean 4)

                
The optimal circuit C* minimises depth subject to the constraint that its unitary U(C) equals the target unitary U_target. The constraint is verified by Lean 4 (formal proof), not just numerical simulation.

              

              
Math → Code derivation (5 steps)

              

                Step 1: Target unitary
                
U_target in SU(2^n) - the desired n-qubit operation

                
The target is a unitary matrix in SU(2^n) - the special unitary group on n qubits. Examples: QFT, Grover diffusion, Hamiltonian simulation.

              

              

                Step 2: Gate set
                
G = {H, CNOT, T, S, ...} - native gates of the quantum processor

                
The available gates are the native gates of the quantum processor (e.g. IBM's ECR + RZ + SX). The circuit is a sequence of these gates.

              

              

                Step 3: LLM mutation
                
child = LLM(parent + target + history)

                
The LLM mutates the circuit: add/remove/reorder gates, replace gate sequences with equivalent decompositions. The mutation must be a valid circuit (no dangling qubits).

              

              

                Step 4: Lean 4 verification
                
Lean 4 proves U(child) = U_target (exact, not numerical)

                
Lean 4 formally verifies that the evolved circuit's unitary equals the target - not just approximately (numerical simulation) but exactly (symbolic proof). This guarantees correctness to machine-precision.

              

              

                Step 5: Depth minimisation
                
depth(C*) = 0.6 * depth(C_human) - 40% improvement

                
The evolved circuit is 40% shallower than the best human design. Shallower circuits have fewer gate errors (each gate introduces ~0.1% error), so the 40% reduction in depth reduces the total error by ~40%.

bci_decode(neural_signal, llm) - real-time neural-to-text with LLM correction

              
Sketch the 2029 vision: decode neural spiking into text in real-time, with an LLM correcting for neural noise and predicting the next word.

            

            ▾
          

          

            

              
              
              
            

            
            

              

                
text(t) = LLM_correct( decode( spike_train(t-dt:t) ) ) + LLM_predict( text(t-dt:t) )

                
The decoded text at time t is the LLM-corrected output of the neural decoder (applied to the recent spike train) PLUS the LLM's prediction of the next word (based on the text so far). The correction handles neural noise; the prediction accelerates typing.

              

              
Math → Code derivation (5 steps)

              

                Step 1: Neural recording
                
s(t) = spike_train from 1024 channels at 30 kHz

                
A 1024-channel implant records spiking activity from the motor cortex at 30 kHz. The patient imagines typing; the motor cortex encodes the intended characters as spiking patterns.

              

              

                Step 2: Feature extraction
                
f(t) = bin_spikes(s(t-dt:t), dt=50ms) - 1024-dim feature vector per 50ms bin

                
Bin the spikes into 50ms windows, producing a 1024-dimensional feature vector per bin. This is the input to the decoder.

              

              

                Step 3: Neural decoder
                
char(t) = softmax(W_dec * f(t) + b) - per-character probability

                
A linear decoder maps the 1024-dim feature vector to a probability distribution over characters. The decoder is trained on (neural_signal, typed_char) pairs from a calibration session.

              

              

                Step 4: LLM correction + prediction
                
text(t) = LLM_correct(char(t)) + LLM_predict(text(t-dt:t))

                
The LLM (Gemini-derived, fine-tuned on the patient's writing style) corrects the noisy decoded character (fixing neural-noise errors) AND predicts the next word (accelerating typing by 2-3x).

              

              

                Step 5: 120 words/min
                
wpm = 50ms_per_char * 5_chars_per_word = 250ms_per_word = 120 wpm

                
At 50ms per character (20 Hz decode rate), with LLM prediction reducing the effective characters-per-word to ~2.5, the throughput is 120 wpm - 3x faster than average keyboard typing (40 wpm).
