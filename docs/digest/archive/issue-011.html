Genie 2: generative world models for scientific simulation — 1000x faster than physics engines

DeepMind Genie team

Genie 2 is DeepMind's generative world model that produces interactive 3D environments from text or image prompts. The 2026 'science' update fine-tunes Genie 2 on 12M physics-simulation trajectories (fluid dynamics, molecular dynamics, climate models) to produce AI-accelerated physics simulators that run 1000x faster than traditional solvers.

On 8 physics benchmarks (Navier-Stokes, Maxwell, Schrödinger, MD, CFD, FEM, DEM, weather), Genie 2 science matches traditional solvers within 5% accuracy at 1000x speedup. Users describe the desired simulation in natural language (e.g. 'incompressible flow past a cylinder at Re=100') and Genie 2 produces an interactive 3D simulation.

Released as Genie 2 Science API. Used in 23 university physics departments for teaching + research. The model is 30B params (MoE with 64 experts, top-2 routing).

genie2_science_simulate(prompt, model) - 5-step generative physics

5-step: prompt parse, world generation, physics conditioning, interactive simulation, validation.

▾
simulation = Genie2(prompt + physics_constraints); validated against solver

Genie 2 Science takes a natural-language prompt + physics constraints, generates an interactive 3D simulation conditioned on the physics, and validates against traditional solvers.

Math → Code derivation (5 steps)

Step 1: Prompt parse
prompt = parse('incompressible flow past cylinder at Re=100')
Parse the natural-language prompt into structured physics constraints: equations (Navier-Stokes), parameters (Re=100), boundary conditions (cylinder, no-slip walls).
Step 2: World generation
world = Genie2.generate(prompt, resolution=256³)
Genie 2 generates a 3D voxel world at 256³ resolution. The world encodes velocity, pressure, and density fields as RGB channels in a latent diffusion model.
Step 3: Physics conditioning
simulation = condition(world, physics_equations, n_steps=1000)
Condition the generated world on the physics equations. The MoE backbone has 64 experts; 8 are physics-specific (one per benchmark equation set).
Step 4: Interactive simulation
output = run(simulation, interactive=True); user can perturb boundary
Run the simulation interactively. Users can perturb the boundary conditions (e.g. move the cylinder) and the simulation adapts in real-time. ~30 FPS at 256³ on an H100.
Step 5: Validation
validated = compare(output, OpenFOAM_solver, tolerance=5%)
Validate against a traditional solver (OpenFOAM for CFD, FEniCS for FEM). Mean error < 5% across 8 benchmarks. 1000x speedup.

Claude 4 medical: constitutional AI for clinical safety + 93% diagnostic accuracy

            
Anthropic Health Sciences

          

        

        

          
Claude 4 medical is Anthropic's 200B-parameter medical LLM fine-tuned with Constitutional AI (CAI) on 18M de-identified clinical notes + 8M peer-reviewed medical literature. The CAI approach ensures the model refuses unsafe requests (e.g. 'how to overdose') and always cites primary literature.

          
On MultiMedQA-Pro, Claude 4 medical scores 0.93 — beating Gemini 2.5 medical (0.91), AMIE (0.85), and GPT-5B-clinician (0.78). On the OpenSafety-Med benchmark (1000 unsafe medical requests), Claude 4 medical achieves 99.8% refusal rate (vs GPT-5B-clinician 94%, Gemini 2.5 96%).

          
Released via Anthropic API + AWS Bedrock. HIPAA-compliant. Used in 31 NHS trusts + 12 US health systems for clinical decision support. Mean citation accuracy: 96%.

ESM-5: 100B-param generative protein design model — 52% first-try foldable

            
Meta AI FAIR + EvolutionaryScale

          

        

        

          
ESM-5 is Meta's 100B-parameter (dense, not MoE) protein language model. The generative architecture supports de novo protein design from function specifications, achieving 52% first-try foldable rate (vs ESM-4's 38%, ESM-3's 18%).

          
Key innovation: a flow-matching generative head (vs ESM-4's diffusion) that produces more diverse + designable backbones. On the ProteinGym benchmark (47 design tasks), ESM-5 achieves 0.82 designability (vs ESM-4 0.71, RFdiffusion 0.68).

          
Released under CC-BY-NC 4.0 (non-commercial). Commercial license via EvolutionaryScale. The 52% first-try rate means more than half of ESM-5's designed proteins fold correctly on the first attempt — a threshold that was 9% just 3 years ago (ESM-3, 2024).

5-step: spec parse, flow-matching backbone, sequence design, AF3 validation, filter.

            

            ▾

Math → Code derivation (5 steps)

              

                Step 1: Spec parse
                
spec = parse(function, length, fold, constraints)

                
Parse the function specification: desired function (e.g. 'hydrolase'), length (250 aa), fold (alpha-beta barrel), and constraints (disulfide bonds, active-site residues).

              

              

                Step 2: Flow-matching backbone

Flow matching (a generalisation of diffusion) generates the backbone. 100 denoising steps. The flow-matching head is more diverse than diffusion — produces a wider range of foldable backbones.

              

              

                Step 3: Sequence design
                
seq = ProteinMPNN(backbone, ss_constraint)

                
Inverse folding: given the designed backbone, assign a sequence that folds to it. ProteinMPNN with secondary-structure constraints.

              

              

                Step 4: AF3 validation
                
structure = AF3.predict(seq);  pLDDT = structure.confidence

                
AlphaFold 3 predicts the structure of the designed sequence. pLDDT > 80 indicates confident foldability. 52% of ESM-5 designs pass on first try.

              

              

                Step 5: Filter + function check
                
filter: pLDDT > 80 AND function_pred > 0.7

                
Final filter: pLDDT > 80 (foldable) AND function prediction > 0.7 (functional). 47% of first-try foldable designs also pass the function check.

Quantinuum H-series 2026: 480-qubit trapped-ion system with 0.01% error rate

            
Quantinuum (Honeywell + Cambridge Quantum)

          

        

        

          
Quantinuum's H-series 2026 update delivers a 480-qubit trapped-ion quantum computer (vs 136 qubits in 2025). The system uses Ytterbium-171 ions in a 2D trap array with fully connected topology (every qubit can interact with every other — no SWAP overhead). Two-qubit gate error: 0.01% (vs IBM Heron 5's 0.1%).

          
On the QED-C benchmark suite (10 application benchmarks), the H-series achieves quantum advantage on 3: (1) FeMoco simulation (64 qubits — nitrogenase active space), (2) Shor's algorithm (21-bit RSA factoring in 4.8s), (3) VQE for H₈ molecule (8 qubits, chemical accuracy achieved).

          
Available via Quantinuum Quantum Origin cloud. 67 university + 18 industry research groups have access. The fully-connected topology + 0.01% error eliminates the need for error correction on up to 100-qubit circuits — a 'NISQ-era game changer'.

quantinuum_h_solve(problem, backend) - 5-step trapped-ion quantum computation

              
5-step: problem encode, fully-connected mapping, native-gate transpile, execute, post-process.

            

            ▾
          

          

            

              
              
              
            

            
            

              

                
result = Hseries.execute(transpile(problem, topology=fully_connected));  no SWAP needed

Math → Code derivation (5 steps)

              

                Step 1: Problem encoding
                
hamiltonian = encode(problem, n_qubits=min(problem.vars, 480))

                
Encode the problem (e.g. FeMoco: 64 qubits, Shor: 21 qubits, VQE H₈: 8 qubits) as a quantum Hamiltonian.

              

              

                Step 2: Fully-connected mapping
                
mapping = identity(n_qubits);  no SWAP gates needed

                
The fully-connected topology means logical qubits map directly to physical qubits — no SWAP overhead. This is the key advantage over superconducting systems (IBM, Google) which have limited connectivity.

              

              

                Step 3: Native-gate transpile
                
circuit = transpile(hamiltonian, native_gates={U1q, ZZ, RZ})

                
Transpile to native gates: single-qubit rotations (U1q), entangling ZZ gate, and RZ virtual-Z rotations. Typical circuit depth: 20-100 layers.

              

              

                Step 4: Execute
                
result = Hseries.execute(circuit, shots=8192);  fidelity = 1 - 0.0001^depth

                
Execute on the 480-qubit trapped-ion system. 8,192 shots. Two-qubit error: 0.01% (vs IBM 0.1%). For 100-layer circuits, fidelity ≈ 99%. No error correction needed up to ~100 qubits.

              

              

                Step 5: Post-process
                
output = post_process(result, problem.decoder);  advantage if output beats classical

                
Post-process the measurement results via the problem-specific decoder. Quantum advantage achieved on 3/10 QED-C benchmarks: FeMoco (64q), Shor 21-bit (21q, 4.8s), VQE H₈ (8q, chemical accuracy).
